\begin{abstract}

This paper examines how the expansion of higher education affects education–job matching in Iran. We combine 18 waves of the Iranian Labor Force Survey from 2007 to 2024 with a newly constructed province–cohort measure of exposure to university expansion, exploiting sharp increases in access to higher education across provinces and cohorts. Among cohorts exposed to the expansion at university-entry ages (18–20), a 10 percent increase in university exposure raises the probability of overeducation by 0.335 percentage points. It also increases horizontal mismatch, defined as working in a job unrelated to one’s field of study, by 0.614 percentage points, and double mismatch, defined as simultaneous overeducation and horizontal mismatch, by 0.695 percentage points. These effects differ substantially by gender. The increases in horizontal and double mismatch are considerably larger among men, whereas employed women exhibit substantially better observed education–job matching. This pattern may reflect stronger selection into employment among women or other gender-specific labor-market frictions. Occupational decompositions show that the rise in double mismatch among men is concentrated in sales, agriculture, and manual occupations, while among women it is concentrated primarily in sales and selected production and craft occupations. The results are robust to alternative definitions of mismatch and to excluding cross-province movers and Tehran. Overall, the findings show that expanding access to higher education without a corresponding expansion in suitable employment opportunities can increase the underutilization of human capital even when aggregate employment changes little.



%{This study exploits province–cohort differences exposure to Iran’s university boom (mid-1990s to mid-2010s) to look at three outcomes: vertical mismatch (working in jobs that need less education), horizontal mismatch (working outside one’s study field), and see how much of the job gender gap in labor market comes from selective occupation choice by women. Linking 18 waves of the Iranian Labour Force Survey during 2007–2024; to a measure of exposure to university boom at ages 18–20, we find that a 10-point rise in exposure boosts vertical mismatch by 2 percentage points and horizontal mismatch by 3, with both effects two-to-three times larger for men. Women appear less mismatched not because they are protected from mismatch, but because only an unusually well-qualified minority of them choose to be employed. Selection alone explains 13.6-hour weekly work-hours gender gap among employees and most of the employment-rate gap. Using a supplementary national data on Konkoor exam takers show women already make up over 61 percent of qualified university applicants, so the main barrier is not a shortage of the educated women. Policies that remove the barriers for women’s work, rather than simply expanding university seats, are needed to turn educational gains into equal employment outcomes.}
\end{abstract}
