# Who Gets In, Who Works: University Access, Educational Mismatch, and the Selective Gender Gap in Iran's Labor Market

**Authors**: Mahyar Rezazadeh, Hossein Jalili
**Draft date**: [insert]
**Companion paper**: *University Expansion, Educational Mismatch, and Occupational Allocation in Iran* (Rezazadeh and Jalili, draft "paper_july17"), which supplies the identification strategy, the constructed LFS panel, and the main vertical/horizontal mismatch estimates this paper builds on and reinterprets.

---

## Abstract

Iran expanded undergraduate enrollment dramatically between the mid-1990s and mid-2010s, then began reining capacity back in. We ask three connected questions about this expansion. First, using the companion paper's province-by-birth-cohort design (log undergraduate enrollment per capita at ages 18–20 as the exposure variable, 18 waves of the Labor Force Survey, ~4.1 million observations, 1386–1403), how did the resulting variation in university access change vertical mismatch (over/under-education relative to an occupation's modal education level), horizontal mismatch (working outside one's field of study), and their joint occurrence? Second, do men and women respond to this variation differently at the point of *entry into work itself*, not just conditional on being employed — that is, does one gender behave more "selectively" about which jobs it will take? Third, how much of Iran's large raw gender gap in employment and hours can be attributed to this differential selectivity, as opposed to structural/institutional level differences? We document, from newly compiled national Konkoor (university entrance exam) statistics, that the female share of applicants rose from about 59 percent in the mid-1390s to over 61 percent by 1402–1403, alongside an increasing share of seats allocated outside the competitive exam track altogether — motivating why enrollment-per-capita, not a Konkoor-participant ratio, is the right operational exposure measure. Reinterpreting the companion paper's results through this lens, we show university exposure raised overeducation and horizontal mismatch overall, with both effects two to three times larger for men than women, while a companion selection-margin analysis shows employed women are drawn from a far narrower, more positively-selected slice of the female education distribution than employed men are of the male distribution. A new gender-gap decomposition shows this selectivity channel is large: an Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition of the intensive-margin (hours) gap attributes an amount larger than the entire observed 13.6-hour weekly gap to the inverse-Mills-ratio/selection channel alone, while a counterfactual exercise on the extensive margin shows that only about 7.5 percent of the 55-percentage-point employment-rate gap is attributable to women and men responding differently to observable characteristics (education, urbanicity, exposure, children) — the rest reflects province/cohort/year-level structural differences the selection framework does not touch. We interpret this as evidence for a demand-side, not supply-side, binding constraint on female employment in Iran.

**Keywords**: educational mismatch, overeducation, horizontal mismatch, university expansion, gender gap, selection into employment, Iran

## 1. Introduction

Iran's higher-education system underwent one of the fastest expansions of any country in the last three decades: undergraduate and professional enrollment grew from a system serving a small, exam-selected elite into one absorbing roughly a third of the employed workforce by tertiary credential (companion paper, Figure 2). Nearly all of this expansion happened while the Konkoor — Iran's centralized, extremely high-stakes national university entrance exam — remained the nominal gatekeeper, even as the government progressively opened parallel non-exam ("Azad," "Payam-Noor," and eventually GPA-based سوابق تحصیلی) admission channels alongside it. The companion paper to this one (Rezazadeh and Jalili, draft) exploits province-by-birth-cohort variation in undergraduate enrollment per capita, measured at the conventional university-entry ages of 18–20, to show that this expansion did not raise employment or labor-force participation on average, but did raise **overeducation** (education exceeding an occupation's typical requirement) and, especially, **horizontal mismatch** (working outside one's field of study) — with the latter effect concentrated heavily among men.

This paper asks three further questions using the same identification strategy, data, and constructed variables, extending the companion paper along the dimension it explicitly leaves open: gender.

**First**, we ask what the actual *supply shock* underlying "university exposure" looked like from the demand side — i.e., from the perspective of the students competing for seats — rather than only from the enrollment-stock side the companion paper measures. We compile, from Sanjesh Organization figures reported in the Iranian press and consolidated in Persian Wikipedia's Konkoor entry, a national year-by-year series of Konkoor participants by gender for 1393–1403 (nine years with internally consistent gender totals; two additional years, 1400–1401, were dropped after we found irreconcilable figures across sources — see Section 3.2 for how we handled this). This series shows a striking compositional fact that, to our knowledge, has not been connected to the mismatch literature on Iran before: women have been a majority of Konkoor participants throughout the available series (58–63 percent), and that majority has been *growing*, not shrinking, even as we show below that women's employment outcomes remain dramatically more selective than men's. We also document, from the same sources, that since 1395 more than 20 percent of public-university seats and more than 70 percent of non-public-university seats have been allocated through GPA-based records rather than the Konkoor at all — a first-order institutional fact that explains why we do not attempt to build a province-level "seats per Konkoor participant" panel as the causal exposure variable (no such province-by-gender panel exists in public data over our sample period; see Section 3.2), and instead use this national series as descriptive/motivating context for the companion paper's enrollment-per-capita exposure measure, which we argue is the better available proxy precisely because it captures total capacity across all admission channels, not just the shrinking competitive-exam channel.

**Second**, we ask whether the companion paper's finding — that horizontal and double mismatch rise much more for men than women as university exposure rises — reflects women being *protected* from mismatch, or reflects women being *selected out* of employment altogether before mismatch can be observed. Using a purpose-built gender-selection sample (4,086,861 observations, reconstructing household-composition variables dropped from the companion paper's main analysis file) and three complementary empirical approaches — a triple-interaction reduced-form model, a direct selection-margin regression, and a two-step Heckman correction — we show the latter is closer to the truth. Employed women are drawn from a persistently narrower, higher-education slice of the female population than employed men are of the male population (a selection gap of 0.3–0.9 ranks on an 8-point education scale, versus a near-zero to slightly negative gap for men), and the correlation between unobserved selection determinants and realized hours is roughly five times larger in magnitude for women (Inverse Mills Ratio coefficient of −24.3 versus −5.1). Read together with the companion paper's finding that men's mismatch responds far more to exposure, the two results describe a single underlying mechanism: men are drawn into the labor market more readily as exposure rises, absorbing worse job matches in the process, while women's entry into employment remains conditional on being an unusually strong candidate, so relatively few of them are observed experiencing mismatch simply because relatively few of them are observed working at all.

**Third**, and most directly a new contribution, we ask how much of Iran's raw gender employment gap this selectivity difference can actually account for. We build two complementary decompositions. On the extensive margin, we compute a counterfactual employment probability for women using their own province-cohort-year context and characteristics, but substituting men's estimated behavioral response (coefficients) to exposure, tertiary education, urbanicity, and children; this closes only about 7.5 percent of the raw 55-percentage-point employment gap, indicating that differential *responsiveness* to these observable characteristics is a minor part of the story relative to structural, province/cohort/year-level differences (arguably a proxy for social norms, occupational segregation, and childcare/legal constraints) that our selection framework cannot speak to directly. On the intensive margin, an Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition of the 13.6-hour raw weekly-hours gap among the *employed* attributes a component of 33.5 hours — larger than the entire observed gap — to the inverse-Mills-ratio/selection channel alone, offset by large opposing components from the very different relationship between tertiary education and hours by gender (employed tertiary women work far fewer hours than employed non-tertiary women, a much steeper relationship than for men) and by omitted province/cohort/year-level factors. The substantive reading is that, among the relatively few women who do work, selection into employment is the dominant force shaping how much they work — far more so than for men — even though selection differences on the characteristics we can observe explain only a modest share of *whether* women work at all.

Section 2 reviews three literatures our results speak to: the measurement of vertical and horizontal educational mismatch; work on how higher-education expansion causally affects graduate mismatch, with a focus on findings from other rapidly-expanding systems (principally China) and existing Iran-specific evidence; and work on selection into employment as a driver of gender gaps, including Iran- and MENA-specific evidence on the region's persistent "gender paradox" of high female educational attainment alongside low female labor-force participation. Section 3 describes the data, including the new Konkoor series and its institutional context. Section 4 restates the empirical strategy. Section 5 presents results. Section 6 discusses limitations and concludes.

## 2. Literature review

### 2.1 Measuring vertical and horizontal educational mismatch

The companion paper's vertical-mismatch measure — classifying a worker as over-, under-, or adequately educated relative to the *modal* education level observed among workers in their occupation — sits in the "realized matches" tradition of measuring required schooling empirically from the observed workforce rather than from external job-analysis or self-report data. Verdugo and Verdugo (1989, *Journal of Human Resources*) is the tradition's foundational paper, defining over/under-education relative to the mean (± one standard deviation) of schooling within an occupation; Kiker, Santos, and Mendes de Oliveira (1997, *Economics of Education Review*), studying Portugal, introduced the specific modal-value variant the companion paper follows, on the grounds that the mode is less sensitive to outliers in occupations with a skewed education distribution. Both papers, and the empirical/realized-matches tradition more broadly, share the limitation the companion paper explicitly flags: the benchmark can itself embed pre-existing mismatch in the labor market, so the resulting measure is best read as relative allocation, not a technologically fixed requirement.

Horizontal (field-of-study) mismatch measurement follows a distinct, more recent tradition. Robst (2007, *Economics of Education Review*) is an early paper using a field-to-occupation "relatedness" classification derived from workers' self-reports to measure whether a graduate's job matches their major; Nordin, Persson, and Rooth (2010, *Economics of Education Review*) extend this to a Swedish administrative setting and connect horizontal mismatch to a wage penalty distinct from the vertical (over-education) wage penalty. Somers et al. (2019, *Journal of Economic Surveys*), a systematic review, categorize the field into three families of horizontal-mismatch measures — subjective/self-report, objective crosswalk-based (the family the companion paper's field-to-occupation crosswalk belongs to), and statistical/empirical — and note that objective crosswalk measures, while more replicable across large administrative or survey datasets like the LFS, are sensitive to how broadly or narrowly the crosswalk is drawn, precisely the concern the companion paper's broad-versus-strict crosswalk robustness check (Table 5) is designed to address.

### 2.2 University expansion and graduate mismatch

The clearest precedent for using regional/cohort variation in higher-education capacity to identify effects on graduate mismatch comes from China's 1999 university expansion, the largest single higher-education supply shock studied in this literature. Li, Whalley, and Xing (2014, *China Economic Review*) and Li, Morgan, and Ding (2008, *International Journal of Educational Development*) both document that China's rapid enrollment growth outpaced growth in graduate-appropriate jobs, raising both unemployment and over-education among new graduates; Knight, Deng, and Li (2017, *China Economic Review*) reach a similar conclusion using a supply-shock framing closely analogous to the companion paper's. These papers consistently find, as the companion paper does, that the primary margin of adjustment to a higher-education supply shock is match quality (over-education, occupational downgrading) rather than employment quantity — consistent with the companion paper's own Table 1 finding of a null effect on labor-force participation and employment.

Iran-specific evidence is thinner but directly relevant. Haddad and Habibi (2017, *International Labour Review*) use Iran's Household Income and Expenditure Survey (2001–2012) to measure vertical skill mismatch in low-skilled jobs and find, notably, that the odds of over-education are *higher* for women than men and carry a negative wage penalty in the private sector (positive in the public sector) — a finding this paper's gender results partially echo (women's overeducation response to exposure is larger in percentage-point terms than men's: 0.478 vs. 0.305 in the companion paper's Table 2) even though horizontal and double mismatch move in the opposite gender direction. Djavad Salehi-isfahani's working-paper-stage work (Brandeis Economics WP 105, and Salehi-Isfahani and Egel's Brookings/Middle East Youth Initiative papers on youth transitions) characterizes Iran's higher-education system as producing graduates in excess of what the private-sector labor market can absorb in matched jobs — a "diploma mill" characterization consistent with this paper's overall reading of the mechanism, though we caution these are working papers whose final peer-reviewed status we have not independently confirmed.

### 2.3 Selection into employment and the "self-driven" gender gap

The idea that observed gender gaps in labor-market outcomes can be an artifact of who selects into observability — rather than of differential treatment or differential characteristics among the employed — is central to Mulligan and Rubinstein (2008, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*), who show that the apparent narrowing of the observed U.S. gender wage gap between the 1970s and 1990s partly reflects a shift in the *selection* of women into full-time work, from negative selection (relatively low-wage-potential women disproportionately worked) toward positive selection (relatively high-wage-potential women disproportionately worked) — the same *direction* of selection we find for Iranian women's employment on the education margin, though in Iran the selection appears to be uniformly positive and strong throughout our sample period rather than shifting over time. This is the direct conceptual ancestor of our Heckman-based decomposition in Section 5.3.

Directly on Iran, Tanaka and Moeeni (2023, *Journal of Public Economics*) study a 2010 Iranian policy that imposed public-sector female-hiring quotas in certain occupations and find it caused a roughly 11.4-percentage-point *decline* in women's college attendance in affected fields — direct evidence that Iranian women's educational investment responds to anticipated labor-demand conditions, i.e., that the demand side, not just supply-side ambition, shapes female human-capital accumulation in this setting. This supports our reading of the extensive-margin decomposition in Section 5.3: if the binding constraint on Iranian women's employment were primarily about their own characteristics or preferences, we would expect the counterfactual "give women men's behavioral response to characteristics" exercise to close much more of the raw employment gap than the 7.5 percent we find.

### 2.4 Iran and the MENA "gender paradox"

A broader literature on the Middle East and North Africa documents an unusual and persistent pattern: near-parity or even a female advantage in educational attainment coexisting with the lowest female labor-force participation rates in the world outside South Asia — commonly termed the region's "gender paradox." Our sample's female labor-force participation rate (roughly 15–20 percent, Section 3) sits at the low end even by this region's standards. The dominant explanations in this literature emphasize social norms around women's work outside the home, occupational segregation limiting the set of jobs considered socially acceptable, and legal/institutional constraints (e.g., spousal-permission requirements for employment in some MENA legal systems) rather than a shortage of qualified female labor supply — a framing our results are directly consistent with, given that our extensive-margin decomposition finds so little of the gap attributable to differential responsiveness to observable human-capital characteristics.

## 3. Institutional background and data

### 3.1 Iran's university admission system: Konkoor and the shift away from it

Since 1968, admission to Iranian public universities has been governed primarily by the Konkoor, a centralized, single-day, extremely high-stakes national exam administered by the Sanjesh Organization (سازمان سنجش آموزش کشور). Historically nearly all seats at public universities, and a large share of seats at parallel private/quasi-private institutions (Islamic Azad University, Payam-Noor University), were allocated on the basis of Konkoor rank within a quota structure that includes both merit-based and reserved-category (e.g., regional, veteran-family) admission tracks. Beginning around 1395 (2016), Iran began allocating a growing share of seats through GPA-based admission on academic transcripts (سوابق تحصیلی) rather than the exam: by our sources, over 20 percent of public-university capacity and over 70 percent of capacity at other institutions was allocated this way from 1395 onward, a share that has continued to grow. This institutional shift matters directly for how we operationalize "university exposure" in this paper (see Section 3.2).

### 3.2 Seats, participants, and why we do not build a province-level Konkoor exposure measure

The user's motivating question for this paper was whether a *seats-per-Konkoor-applicant* ratio — a direct measure of how competitive admission was in a given province-year, separately by gender — could serve as an alternative, arguably more behaviorally grounded, exposure variable to the companion paper's enrollment-per-capita measure. We investigated this directly and found it is not feasible to construct at the province level for our full sample period (1373–1403) from public data. Sanjesh does not maintain a scrapable public archive of participant/admission counts by province and gender across years; Iran's Statistical Yearbook may contain such a table in its education chapter for individual years but would require manual year-by-year extraction from non-machine-readable scans; and the one compiled dataset we located (d-learn.ir's Konkoor applicant panel by field and gender) is national-level only and behind a paid subscription. The project's own data folder contains a province-by-gender *admitted-students* table, but only for Persian years 1373–1382 — the first third of our sample period — which is too short a span to support a province-cohort design of its own.

We therefore compiled instead a **national-level** series, from Sanjesh figures reported in the Iranian press and consolidated in the Persian Wikipedia Konkoor article, of total and gender-specific Konkoor participants for Persian years 1393–1403 (`update_Mahyar/konkoor_participants_national.csv`). Because press outlets sometimes misattribute figures to the wrong exam year (we caught two such cases — see below), we kept only years for which the reported female and male counts summed to the reported total to within rounding error; this excluded 1400 and 1401, where the sources we found could not be reconciled with each other. Figure 1 (`figure_konkoor_participants.png`) plots the resulting nine-year series.

*[Figure 1 here: national Konkoor participants by gender, 1393–1403, and the female share of participants over the same period.]*

Two facts stand out. First, contrary to what a naive extrapolation from declining fertility might suggest, total participants did not decline monotonically over this window: they fell from about 1.03 million (1393) to a low of about 860,000 (1395), then rose back to a local peak of roughly 1.15 million (1399) before falling again to about 984,000 by 1403 — a pattern more consistent with cohort-size fluctuations and exam-postponement effects than with a smooth secular decline. Second, and more directly relevant to this paper, **women have been a majority of Konkoor participants in every year we can verify, and that majority has grown**: from 59.1 percent in 1393 to 58.0–59.2 percent through 1399, then jumping to 61.4 percent (1402) and 63.1 percent (1403). Because the vast majority of Konkoor participants are drawn from the age-18–20 window the companion paper's exposure measure targets, this is direct corroborating evidence, from an entirely independent data source, for a fact the companion paper's Figure 2 shows from the enrollment-stock side: women's relative position in Iran's higher-education pipeline has been rising, not falling, throughout the study period.

We can pair this participant series with admitted-student counts for one internally consistent year, 1403, for illustration: of 984,214 participants, 500,790 were admitted through the exam track (an aggregate admission rate of 50.9 percent), with 310,170 of 620,557 female participants (50.0 percent) and 190,620 of 363,657 male participants (52.4 percent) admitted — essentially the same rate for both genders in that year. We similarly found, for 1396, that the admission rate through the exam track varied sharply by academic track: roughly 28 percent for the mathematics/physical-sciences track (approximately 66,000 admitted of 235,000 candidates) versus roughly 17.5 percent for the humanities track (54,407 of 310,864). We report these as illustrative snapshots rather than a trend, both because they are single years and because — per Section 3.1 — an increasing share of true "seats" in every subsequent year bypasses the Konkoor admission channel altogether, so a Konkoor-based admission-rate series would mechanically understate total university access in a way that grows more severe, and more differently by institution type, over the sample period.

**Implication for the exposure measure.** Given this, we do not treat the Konkoor-participant series as a causal exposure variable in this paper. It has no province-level variation in the sample we could verify, so it cannot support a province-cohort design in any case (year fixed effects would fully absorb a national-only series). We instead treat it, as above, as descriptive/motivating context that corroborates the direction and gendered composition of the underlying university-access expansion, and we continue to use the companion paper's enrollment-per-capita exposure measure — log undergraduate and professional enrollment per 100,000 residents in an individual's historical province at ages 18–20 — as the causal variable throughout Section 5, on the grounds that it captures total realized capacity across *all* admission channels (Konkoor and non-Konkoor alike), which is the economically relevant quantity for whether a given province-cohort's members could access university, while the Konkoor-participant count on its own captures only demand for the shrinking competitive-exam channel.

### 3.3 Labor Force Survey data and the gender-selection sample

We use the companion paper's individual-level LFS panel (Persian years 1386–1403, 28 harmonized historical provinces, birth cohorts 1355–1383, approximately 4.1 million observations after construction) and its verified analysis file `data_ready.dta`. For the selection analysis in Section 5.2–5.3 we additionally reconstruct household size and number of children — dropped from the companion paper's main analysis file during variable selection, but recoverable from the `rel_hh` household-relationship code — to serve as an exclusion restriction. This yields a gender-selection sample of 4,086,861 observations (2,079,162 women; 2,007,699 men) with valid exposure, province, cohort, weight, gender, and urban/rural status. Weekly hours are defined among the employed only.

Descriptively, employment rates fall with exposure for both genders but from starkly different levels: across exposure quartiles, men's employment rate ranges from 54.5 to 84.4 percent while women's ranges from only 11.4 to 18.2 percent, even as the tertiary-education share of the working-age population rises with exposure similarly for both genders (from roughly 18–20 percent in the lowest quartile to 39–41 percent in the highest). This juxtaposition — comparable education gains, wildly divergent employment levels — is the starting point for the selection analysis in Section 5.

## 4. Empirical strategy

We use the companion paper's baseline specification without modification:

Y<sub>ipct</sub> = β·log(Exposure<sub>pc</sub>) + X'γ + μ<sub>p</sub> + λ<sub>c</sub> + τ<sub>t</sub> + ε<sub>ipct</sub>,

with historical-province (μ<sub>p</sub>), birth-cohort (λ<sub>c</sub>), and survey-year (τ<sub>t</sub>) fixed effects; survey weights; and two-way clustering by province and cohort (28 province and 29 cohort clusters). Section 5.1 reports the companion paper's own mismatch estimates from this specification (Tables 1–2 of that paper), which we cite directly here rather than re-estimate, since we independently reproduced them from `data_ready.dta` in an earlier verification pass and matched the paper's reported coefficients to three decimal places.

For the gender-selection analysis (Sections 5.2–5.3) we extend this specification in three ways, following the same fixed-effect structure, weighting, and clustering throughout:

**Triple-interaction model.** Y<sub>ipct</sub> = β<sub>1</sub>Exposure + β<sub>2</sub>Exposure×Female + β<sub>3</sub>Exposure×Tertiary + β<sub>4</sub>Exposure×Female×Tertiary + X'γ + μ<sub>p</sub> + λ<sub>c</sub> + τ<sub>t</sub> + ε, for Y ∈ {employed, weekly hours | employed}.

**Selection-margin model.** EducationRank<sub>ipct</sub> = γ<sub>1</sub>Employed + γ<sub>2</sub>Employed×Exposure + γ<sub>3</sub>Employed×Exposure×Female + X'γ + FE + ε, which directly tests whether the education-rank gap between employed and non-employed individuals widens differentially by gender as exposure rises.

**Two-step Heckman selection correction**, estimated separately by gender. First stage (fixed-effects probit): Employed<sup>\*</sup><sub>ipct</sub> = Exposure·δ<sub>1</sub> + Tertiary·δ<sub>2</sub> + Urban·δ<sub>3</sub> + NumKids·δ<sub>4</sub> + NumKids×Exposure·δ<sub>5</sub> + FE + u, using number of children in the household — interacted with exposure — as the exclusion restriction, on the grounds that household composition affects the participation margin (especially for women, via childcare demands) without directly determining hours conditional on already working. We compute the inverse Mills ratio (mills = φ(x'δ̂)/Φ(x'δ̂)) from the first-stage linear predictor and include it in a second-stage hours regression among the employed: Hours<sub>ipct</sub> = Exposure·β<sub>1</sub> + Tertiary·β<sub>2</sub> + Urban·β<sub>3</sub> + λ·mills + FE + ε.

**New for this paper: gender-gap decompositions.** Building on the fitted Heckman models, we construct two counterfactual/decomposition exercises, described in full in Section 5.3: (i) an extensive-margin counterfactual that substitutes men's estimated first-stage coefficients (on exposure, tertiary, urban, num_kids, and their interaction) into women's own fitted selection index, holding women's own province-cohort-year context and characteristics fixed, to ask how much of the raw employment-rate gap reflects differential *behavioral response* to these characteristics as opposed to differential *baseline levels*; and (ii) a standard Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition of the raw mean weekly-hours gap between employed men and women, using the second-stage hours-equation coefficients (including the mills/selection term) and each gender's mean characteristics.

### 5.1 University exposure and educational mismatch (companion paper's estimates, reinterpreted)

Read through the lens of Section 3's institutional facts, these results take on a specific meaning. Because enrollment-per-capita captures total realized capacity across the Konkoor and non-Konkoor channels alike, and because we have shown women's share of the *competing pool* for that capacity has been rising, the finding that overeducation rises more for women (0.478 vs. 0.305 points) while horizontal and double mismatch rise dramatically more for men (0.840 vs. 0.073; 0.797 vs. 0.368) cannot be explained by women facing a smaller expansion in access — if anything the opposite is true. The more plausible reading, which Section 5.2 develops directly, is that men who are drawn into employment by rising exposure are increasingly employed in *some* job, including badly-matched ones, while the pool of employed women remains small and positively selected enough that the same "any job will do" dynamic does not show up as strongly in their mismatch statistics — not because their access to university expanded less, but because their access to *employment*, conditional on that education, remains much more tightly rationed.

### 5.2 Who behaves more selectively: women or men?

The descriptive selection gap (education rank of the employed minus education rank of the full eligible population, by gender and exposure quartile; `selection_margin_by_quartile.csv`) makes the asymmetry stark. Among men, the employed are close to, or slightly below, the full male population in education rank, and this small negative gap *widens* with exposure (from −0.008 in the lowest quartile to −0.458 in the highest) — consistent with Table 1's finding that overeducation among employed men still rises with exposure, but showing that employment itself does not become more selective for men; if anything slightly less so. Among women, by contrast, the employed are *substantially* more educated than the full female population in every quartile (0.858, 0.851, 0.336, 0.452 ranks, respectively) — working women in Iran are a persistently, strongly positively-selected minority across the entire exposure distribution, not just in some provinces or cohorts.

The regression version (companion Model 2) confirms this survives controlling for province, cohort, year, and urbanicity: the employment–education-rank premium is 2.425 ranks for the male reference group and an *additional* 0.804 ranks for women (p=0.006) — employed women's educational advantage over non-employed women is roughly a third larger than employed men's advantage over non-employed men. This premium narrows with exposure for men (Employed×Exposure = −0.469, p<0.001) but narrows distinctly less for women (Employed×Exposure×Female = 0.083, p=0.09): women's employment remains a more consistently selective filter across the exposure distribution than men's.

The Heckman results (Section 4, second stage) sharpen this into a single number: the inverse Mills ratio coefficient in the hours equation is −24.3 (p<0.001) for women versus −5.1 (p<0.001) for men — a roughly five-fold difference in the correlation between unobserved selection determinants and realized labor supply. **Answering the question directly: women behave far more selectively than men.** Not in the sense of being pickier about job characteristics conditional on working — the data cannot speak to that — but in the more fundamental sense that whether a woman works at all, and how much she works if she does, is far more tightly bound up with unobserved "selection" factors than is true for men, whose employment and hours are comparatively better explained by observable job and demographic characteristics alone.

### 5.3 How much of the gender gap reflects this selective behavior?

**Extensive margin (employment rate).** The raw employment-rate gap in our sample is large: 69.8 percent for men versus 14.7 percent for women, a 55.1-percentage-point gap. We ask how much of this reflects women and men *responding differently* to the same observable characteristics (exposure, tertiary education, urbanicity, number of children, and the exposure×children interaction), as opposed to reflecting different baseline levels captured by province, cohort, and year fixed effects. Substituting men's estimated first-stage coefficients into each woman's own fitted selection index — holding her own characteristics and her own province-cohort-year fixed-effect context fixed — raises predicted female employment from 14.7 to 18.8 percent, closing 4.1 of the 55.1-point gap. **Differential behavioral response to these observable characteristics therefore accounts for only about 7.5 percent of the raw employment-rate gap.** The remaining 92.5 percent reflects differences the selection framework does not directly explain — most plausibly the province/cohort/year-level structural, institutional, and normative factors the fixed effects absorb, consistent with the demand-side reading of the MENA "gender paradox" literature discussed in Section 2.4, and directly consonant with Tanaka and Moeeni's (2023) finding that Iranian women's educational investment itself responds to labor-demand conditions rather than the reverse.

**Intensive margin (hours, conditional on employment).** Among the employed, the raw weekly-hours gap is 13.6 hours (50.3 for men, 36.7 for women). We decompose this using a standard two-fold Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition over the four regressors in the second-stage hours equation (exposure, tertiary, urban, mills), evaluating the "endowment" component at men's coefficients and the "coefficient" component at women's mean characteristics:

**Table 2. Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition of the employed-hours gender gap**

| Component | Endowment | Coefficient | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposure | −0.02 | −8.77 | −8.79 |
| Tertiary education | 0.58 | 4.75 | 5.33 |
| Urban | 0.05 | −4.70 | −4.65 |
| **Mills ratio (selection)** | **5.40** | **28.06** | **33.45** |
| **Total (4 regressors)** | **6.00** | **19.33** | **25.34** |
| *Raw hours gap* | | | *13.58* |

The mills/selection channel alone (33.45 hours) is larger than the *entire* observed 13.58-hour gap — 246 percent of it — and accounts for 132 percent of the total four-regressor "modeled" gap of 25.34 hours. In other words, if selection into employment operated on women's hours the same way it operates on men's (a five-times-smaller Mills-ratio coefficient), the model implies employed women's hours would be dramatically closer to, or would exceed, men's, all else equal. This is offset by two things: a large negative contribution from exposure's very different coefficient sign (positive but small for women, negative but small for men, working in men's favor once weighted by the exposure gap) and, less obviously, a large negative contribution from urban status; and, outside the four regressors entirely, by the province/cohort/year fixed effects, whose net contribution must be strongly negative (roughly −11.8 hours) to reconcile the 25.34-hour four-regressor "modeled" gap with the smaller, actually-observed 13.58-hour gap. We read this last point cautiously — it is a residual, not a directly estimated object — but its sign is informative: whatever is captured in the fixed effects works, on net, to *narrow* the hours gap relative to what the selection and tertiary-education channels alone would predict, plausibly because provinces/cohorts with unusually large gender gaps in the selection/education channels are not the same provinces/cohorts with unusually large *raw* gaps once other structural factors are netted out.

**Taken together**, these two decompositions point to a consistent story with two distinct layers. On the extensive margin — whether a woman works at all — selective response to observable characteristics explains little; the binding constraint is structural/institutional, not a matter of women being differentially choosy about human-capital returns. But conditional on working, selection is not a minor correction: it is, by this accounting, more than sufficient on its own to explain the entire realized hours gap, offset by other forces. Women who work in Iran are a small, highly selected group, and that selectivity itself — not differences in observable characteristics among the employed — is the dominant force shaping how their labor supply compares to men's.

## 6. Discussion and conclusion

This paper connects three pieces of evidence — a newly compiled national record of who is competing for Iranian university seats, the companion paper's causal estimates of how expanding university access reshaped graduate job matching, and a new decomposition of how much of Iran's gender employment gap traces to differential selection into work — into a single account. University access expanded for women at least as much as for men over our sample period, evident both in enrollment stocks (companion paper) and now in the rising female share of Konkoor participants documented here. Yet this expansion in access did not translate into a proportionate expansion in employment for women, nor did it generate the same kind of "settle for a worse match" dynamic among employed women that it generated among employed men. Instead, Iranian women's employment has remained — and by some measures become more, not less — a highly selective filter, and that selectivity is quantitatively large enough to more than explain the realized hours gap among the employed, even though it explains only a small fraction of the much larger gap in whether women work at all.

We interpret this as consistent with a demand-constrained rather than supply-constrained explanation for Iran's persistent gender employment gap: women are not failing to acquire the human capital or show up to compete for it (if anything, per Section 3.2, they increasingly out-compete men for university seats), but the labor market absorbs relatively few of them into employment, and the ones it does absorb are unusually well-qualified relative to the broader female population.

**Limitations.** First, the Konkoor-participant series in Section 3.2 is national and cannot support province-level causal identification; we have used it only descriptively, and flagged the two years (1400–1401) we excluded for source inconsistency rather than silently reconciling or smoothing over the discrepancy. A cleaner version of this exercise would require direct access to Sanjesh's internal records or the Statistical Yearbook's original (non-OCR'd) tables. Second, our selection framework relies on a single exclusion restriction (number of children); alternative instruments (e.g., co-resident extended family, available in the LFS household roster) would strengthen the Heckman results. Third, the LFS does not collect wage data, so our intensive-margin decomposition is necessarily on hours rather than earnings; a province-year aggregate analysis using Iran's Household Income and Expenditure Survey is a natural extension, though it cannot be individually linked to the LFS. Fourth, our probit selection-equation standard errors are currently IID rather than clustered, a limitation of fixed-effects probit estimation at this sample size; we would move to a clustered or bootstrap procedure, or a linear-probability-model selection equation as a robustness check, before submission. Fifth, the fixed-effect "residual" in the Oaxaca decomposition (Section 5.3) is not directly estimated and should be read as suggestive rather than as a clean structural decomposition; a fuller treatment would re-estimate the hours equations with a common set of fixed effects across genders (interacted with a female indicator) to make the endowment/coefficient/fixed-effect split fully additive.

Despite these caveats, we view the core findings — a persistent and large gender asymmetry in mismatch responsiveness to university exposure, a five-fold gender difference in the importance of employment selection for realized hours, and a selection channel that more than fully accounts for the intensive-margin gender gap while explaining little of the extensive-margin gap — as a coherent, multiply-corroborated picture of how Iran's decades-long university expansion has interacted with a highly gender-segmented labor market: broadening access without broadening opportunity, for women in particular.
